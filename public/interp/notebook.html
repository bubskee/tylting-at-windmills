The Skeleton Lens

Vishesh’s ideas got us wondering how cheaply we could read a model’s workspace. We used forward passes on 32 passages to group similar activations, then fitted a mixture of those groups on 64 development prompts. On one A40, collecting the coefficients took 14.6 seconds after loading the model. Fitting the mixtures took another 21.0 seconds, excluding setup. Coefficient collection · Fit timings

Revisiting ACE

If you change how a model represents a name halfway through a sentence, can you predict what happens at the final token? Joshua Schiffman fitted a map on ordinary sentence pairs, then tested it by editing the repeated name in held-out sentences. Across six models, the map explained 61–87% of the measured change in the selected final-token coordinates. That prediction is what drew us to ACE. Schiffman’s paper, §6 and Table A11

We reproduced the GPT-2 Small search and gave a simpler baseline the same pairs of sentences with the names’ roles swapped. A basis fitted to the activation differences could preserve or flip the preferred name nearly as well. ACE still had an edge when we removed the fitted directions. At the layers and ranks chosen for ACE, removal brought name preference closer to chance in all five models we tested. Recorded controls

Do different models arrive at the same algorithm? In our smaller transfer check across three GPT-2 sizes, a map fitted directly on the target model beat the transferred map in four of six directions, given the same calibration pairs. The paper’s stronger claim about a shared algorithm remains open.
