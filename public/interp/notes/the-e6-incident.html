OthelloGPT learned from lists of moves. Flip one square on its internal board and the rules tell you exactly which moves should gain or lose support. Follow G6 in our replay. A move can become illegal on the new board and still look perfectly playable to the model.

Historical studyLi et al. · Emergent World Representations, Fig. 2Zoo experiment · saved rows

Published experiment. A probe-guided edit flips OthelloGPT’s internal square E6 from white to black while the move history stays fixed. The model’s move predictions shift toward the legal moves on the edited board. OthelloGPT learned from move histories. That made it possible to ask a concrete question about a sequence model: if it had built an internal board, could changing a single square change which moves it expected next? The rules let you see exactly where the answer should change. The published edit belongs to OthelloGPT. Its place here is as a landmark in interpretability. Li et al., Figure 2 ↗

Proposed range test. Give a class-mean direction a turn on unseen boards. Bet on squares that become legal, become illegal, and stay legal. A rules engine settles every bet. Proposed readout. read the middle layers in move tokens and watch when a newly legal square climbs the rankings.
